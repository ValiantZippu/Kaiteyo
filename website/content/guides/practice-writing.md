Reading kanji is recognition; writing kanji is ownership. Kaiteyo's writing practice turns handwriting into a graded, trackable skill — every stroke evaluated in real time, every attempt feeding your statistics. Here's how to use it properly.

## What the evaluator checks

When you write a kanji on the practice board, the evaluator grades each stroke against the reference stroke data (from KanjiVG):

- **Order** — the sequence of strokes (stroke 2 before stroke 1 is wrong)
- **Direction** — the direction each stroke travels
- **Start/end position** — where the stroke begins and ends
- **Shape** — the path of the stroke, not just its endpoints

This is a *real* evaluation, not a "you drew it, good job" check. See the [stroke grading FAQ](/faq/#faq-stroke-grading) for how it works under the hood.

## Strictness: the dial nobody talks about

The evaluator has a **strictness setting** — Relaxed, Normal, Exam:

- **Relaxed** — for first contact. You're learning *where* the stroke goes, not being judged.
- **Normal** — the default for study. Correct order and reasonable shape.
- **Exam** — strict grading, the same bar used in exams.

The right move: start Relaxed or Normal, and save Exam for when you're testing yourself. Moving from Normal to Exam is a satisfying graduation.

## The writing habit

- **Write new kanji at least once** when you first learn them — the motor memory is built in those first few attempts.
- **Write the ones you keep failing** in reviews. Recognition failure and writing failure are different failure modes; the second is easier to fix with the board.
- **Use the writing card type in study** so writing appears in your normal review queue, not as a separate chore.

## What makes a stroke "good"

Real handwriting doesn't match the reference pixel-perfectly — neither should yours. The evaluator's tolerance is what makes the app teach instead of punish: a stroke that starts and ends in the right region, travels in the right direction, and has the right general shape passes. Write with normal confidence, not obsessive tracing.

## Tracking your progress

Writing attempts and writing accuracy appear in your statistics. If you're practicing regularly, you'll see writing accuracy improve deck by deck — and it's honest feedback on the skill you're building, separate from reading recognition.
