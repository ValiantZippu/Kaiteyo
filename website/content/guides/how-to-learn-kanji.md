There are thousands of kanji, which sounds like a wall. But you don't need thousands — you need the first two hundred done properly, and then the system carries you. This guide is how to learn kanji *with* Kaiteyo's loop: see it, write it, meet it again.

## How kanji actually work

A kanji is not a picture of a word. It's a character with:

- **Meanings** — 山 means *mountain*
- **Readings** — an *on* reading (Chinese-derived, サン) and often one or more *kun* readings (native Japanese, やま)
- **A radical** — a component that hints at meaning or grouping
- **A stroke order** — a fixed, graded sequence for writing

Kaiteyo shows all of these on the kanji detail screen and in every card. You never have to go hunting for the information.

## The learning loop

**Step 1 — meet the kanji in a deck.** The bundled JLPT decks are the right order: N5 first. Add a handful of new cards per day; the scheduler decides when to show them again.

**Step 2 — write it.** For kanji you're serious about, use the writing card type. The stroke evaluator checks order, direction, start/end position and shape, with configurable strictness. Writing a kanji once is worth ten look-ups.

**Step 3 — meet it in vocabulary.** 日 alone is abstract; 日本 (Japan) makes it concrete. Every word links back to the kanji it contains, so vocabulary study reinforces kanji and vice versa. This cross-linking is the biggest force-multiplier in the whole system.

**Step 4 — meet it in the wild.** Subtitles, browser, text hook — when you look up a word and recognise a kanji you've studied, it sticks for good. This is what the media and mining tools are for.

## Radicals: the cheat code

Characters aren't random. 語 (*language*) contains 言 (speech) and 吾. 飲 (*drink*) contains 食 (eat). When you learn radicals as building blocks, new kanji stop being random squiggles and start being recognisable assemblies. The app lets you search and browse by radical — use it.

## How many per day?

Sustainably? **5–10 new kanji per day** is a strong, repeatable pace — 100–150 per month, N5 cleared in a few months. More than that and reviews snowball faster than you can handle. Fewer is completely fine; this is a marathon.

## The uncomfortable truth

Kanji look identical at first. 未, 末, 来 — you *will* mix them up, and that's how learning works: your brain needs the confusion to build the distinction. Don't fight it. The scheduler is literally designed to show you the cards you're about to forget, which is the mix-up you need to see again.

## Writing everything is optional

Some learners write every kanji; some write almost none. Be honest about which you are. If you never write, you'll still read — but the writing loop turns recognition into ownership, and it feeds your writing accuracy statistics. Try it for the first N5 deck and decide with data.
